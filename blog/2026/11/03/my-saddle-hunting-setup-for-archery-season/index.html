My saddle hunting setup for archery season

The saddle, ropes, sticks, platform, and pack I use for a deer management archery program, including what works and what I am still adjusting.
By James Cacioppo | Tuesday, November 03, 2026

I’m a member of a deer management archery program. One of the critical requirements is shots must be taken from at least 10 feet off the ground. That means figuring out how to get myself and my gear into a tree. For me, that means saddle hunting.

I’m not a huge fan of ladder stands and ther’s zero chance you’ll ever find me in one of those climbers. I just don’t trust equipment that’s being left out all season and those climbers scare the crap out of me. Plus, I prefer the mobility of saddle hunting, especially somewhere that’s new to me.

Scouting is limited in this program, and without much time to scout before the season, it’s hard to know where I should set up. I want the flexibility to choose a suitable tree once I have a better idea of where I need to be. Every time I’ve gone into the woods, I’ve picked a different tree to climb because my awareness of what the deer are doing has improved. When I was scouting this season, I found what looked like a high traffic deer highway that followed an elevation contour and then crossed over a point. Literally the stuff you hear and read about. After two sits I realized the location was dead and moved on.

There’s also the cost. If you’re using stands, you need one stand for each location which adds up fast. It’s also hard and noisy to move them mid season if you realize a location isn’t producing like you originally thought it would.

With that in mind, here’s what I’m using, what I like about it, and what I’m still figuring out.

The saddle and storage pockets

My saddle is the Tethrd Phantom XP, and I love it. So far, I have zero complaints about the saddle itself. I also have Tethrd knee pads, which stay in my pack with the saddle until I get to my tree. The saddle is quite comfortable. I haven’t yet sat in it all day, but I have sat in it for four hours. To be clear, I prefer to stand or lean. I find sitting to be hard on my knees and legs and I think I move a lot more when I’m sitting. But, no matter which position I’m in, I’m quite comfortable.

This is a new setup for me. The saddle was just released this year. At this point, I think I have it mostly dialed in. I have a Tethrd SYS Hauler ES2.0 pocket on each side, one for my lineman’s rope and one for my tether.

On the left side I keep my lineman’s belt attached to the saddle and stowed in the SYS Hauler. Under the lineman’s belt is the cam buckle and strap for the platform. This works because my lineman’s belt is out of the pocket and wrapped around the tree whenever I need to retrieve or stow the platform strap.

On the right side I keep the tether. Under the tether is the HYS strap. Similar to the other pocket setup, any time the HYS strap needs to be retrieved or stowed the tether is wrapped around the tree.

I have two small complaints about the pockets. First, the drawstring closures make getting the ropes back in a little fiddly. Second, the smaller pockets on the outside aren’t deep enough to securely hold my HYS strap or the platform’s cam buckle and strap. This is why I put them under the ropes. It works, but I’d rather have dedicated pockets for these items.

Why I chose the Latitude ropes

I use the Latitude 8mm Vapor Line lineman’s belt and tree tether. The feature that sold me on them was the included prusik tender.

A prusik is a friction hitch used to adjust the rope system. The tender helps move that hitch along the rope with one hand. That one-handed adjustment is the convenience I wanted. Latitude supplies both the lineman’s belt and tree tether configured for single-handed operation.

A lot of folks use a mechanical ascender for this purpose, such as the Kong Duck, which replaces the prusik knot. For me, the appeal to the prusik tender is avoiding an additional mechanical device, along with the extra metal that could make noise or add weight.

The Predator V platform

The Tethrd Predator V is a little small for my liking once I’m up in the tree. I’d prefer more room underfoot. I wear a size 12.5, so I find the platform a little uncomfortable at times. I do a lot of rock climbing, so I’m used to having only a small part of my foot on a hold, but it can be clunky and difficult to turn around stealthily to get an angle on an unsuspecting deer.

On the walk in, though, that size is useful. It fits inside my First Lite Transfer pack, and I’m not sure how I’d fit a larger platform in there with everything else. The pack is designed so that you can remove the it from the frame and sandwich a platform between the frame and pack. However, it’s clearly meant for larger platforms, like the Timber Ninja Kunai. I don’t know if the extra-large Predator V will be big enough, but I’ll certainly provide updates when I get a chance to try it.

The Predator V is also a little loud and needs Stealth Strips to quiet it down. This is normal for metal platforms and sticks, but worth noting as the industry starts releasing things made of carbon.

One of the most important factors for a platform is stability. It’s all well and good to make a platform that sticks when you’re just standing on it. A platform for saddle hunting needs to be stable no matter what angle you’re kicking it from. If I lean out to my left, hanging from the tether and resting my feet against the left side of the platform, I need to know that it’s not going to kick out on me. Thus far, the Predator V has not dissapointed me. As I’ve gotten more familiar with deploying it, it’s gotten even more stable.

The Carbon Synergy climbing sticks

Safety update, November 3, 2026: Latitude issued a stop-use notice for all Carbon Synergy climbing sticks on November 2 after reports of steps slipping or detaching from the posts. The company instructs owners to stop using them immediately and offers a full refund regardless of where they were purchased. The observations below describe my experience before this notice.

My Latitude Carbon Synergy climbing sticks have been great to climb with. They’re extremely light, feel sturdy, and have plenty of grip.

One of my favorite things about them is the sound. If they knock into something, the carbon construction gives them more of a rattling-antler sound. They don’t have that metal twang.

Getting them attached to the tree is easy. Getting the attachment cord loose during teardown is harder. I haven’t quite figured out the trick to that part yet.

I either carry the sticks by hand or strap them underneath the Transfer pack. The pack has MOLLE-like webbing on the bottom, so I rigged up straps using one-inch webbing, tri-glides, and G-hooks. That gives me a way to carry the sticks on the pack when I want my hands free.

Carrying the sticks up the tree

For carrying the sticks while climbing, I borrowed an idea from Latitude. I tied a piece of 550 paracord to a loop of MOLLE webbing on my saddle using a double anchor knot. At the other end, I made a loop with a slip knot. That loop slips over the end of a step, letting the stack of sticks hang from the saddle.

This has worked well, but the sticks swing freely. I like that they generally hang clear of my other gear. The downside is that they can make noise if they swing into something. I’d like them to hang a little tighter to me so they don’t move around as much.

The next variation I want to try is tying a loop around one of the sticks and clipping it to an S-biner that lives on my saddle. I haven’t tested that arrangement yet, but I want to see whether it keeps the stack closer and cuts down on the swinging.

Hanging the bow

I use the Tethrd HYS strap with the Tethrd Fold N Go to hold my bow once I’m in the tree. It’s another part of the setup that packs away for the walk in, with the HYS strap currently living underneath a rope in one of the SYS Haulers.

Why I carry the Transfer pack

The First Lite Transfer is a little big and heavy. It’s probably more pack than many people would carry for this kind of hunt, but it works well for how I want to enter these parks.

I want to look as inconspicuous as possible. For me, that means everything goes in or on the pack. I strap my bow to it, keep my midlayers and insulation inside, and pack the saddle and knee pads instead of wearing them on the walk in. Those go on when I reach my tree.

Along with the hunting setup, I carry:

  • A tourniquet and small first aid kit
  • Extra 550 paracord and a carabiner
  • A kill kit
  • Snacks and a Nalgene for all-day sits

Between that gear, the platform, and the clothing, the space gets used. The pack’s size makes more sense when you consider that it’s carrying the saddle and layers as well as the things I need during the hunt.

What I am still adjusting

The saddle itself has been great. The things I’m still working on are mostly about packing, organization, and teardown: where the straps belong, how to make repacking the ropes less fiddly, and how to release the sticks’ attachment cords more easily. The platform needs quieting down, and I’d still like more room to stand.

For now, this setup gives me the mobility I wanted. I can carry everything to my tree, get set up there, and take the same equipment with me when I need to hunt somewhere else.
