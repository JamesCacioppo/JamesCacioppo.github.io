Archery hunting is hard

Cold, tiring sits and a buck within range that never offers a shot. The part of archery hunting that’s hard to capture on camera.
By James Cacioppo | Monday, November 09, 2026

Archery hunting is hard. Sometimes it just sucks.

YouTube and other hunting media make it look easier than it feels. Even when they show the difficulties, the cold mornings and unsuccessful sits still fit into a video you can watch from a warm couch. You know how long the video is. You can skip ahead. In the woods, you just keep waiting.

It’s tiring. You’re cold. You’ve been trying to stay still for hours, and every little sound gets your attention. Eventually, the squirrels start to piss you off.

Then a monster buck comes within range. He’s the biggest thing you’ve ever seen. You might actually get something you’d be proud to hang on the wall. Suddenly, you’re wide awake. He’s walking straight towards you. This could actually happen. You come to full draw the moment no one is looking.

Except there’s no shot. The angle isn’t there. You wait for him to take one more step, turn just a little, give you an opening. Instead, he makes a big, slow circle. When he finally presents you with a perfect 15-yard broadside shot, you’re surrounded by wary does, and turning will give you away. You stand there, as motionless as stone, heart pounding in your ears, waiting for him to look away just for a moment so you can turn and take the shot. But he just stands there, and eventually your arms start to shake. Your bow cams over, the arrow bounces in the rest, and he sees all of it. He blows and runs off.

It’s disheartening, watching him run off. You replay the events in your head for days. Waiting was the right call. You go back over and over, moving from one tree to the next, trying to play the wind, hoping for another chance. You never see him again, and every other buck seems to be just out of reach or too young. It becomes disheartening, and you start to ask yourself if missing your son’s hockey game for this is really worth it.

I don’t have some big lesson to wrap this up with. I just want to remind myself, and anyone reading this, that archery hunting is hard and sometimes it just sucks.

My saddle hunting setup for archery season

The saddle, ropes, sticks, platform, and pack I use for a deer management archery program, including what works and what I am still adjusting.
By James Cacioppo | Tuesday, November 03, 2026

I’m a member of a deer management archery program. One of the critical requirements is shots must be taken from at least 10 feet off the ground. That means figuring out how to get myself and my gear into a tree. For me, that means saddle hunting.

I’m not a huge fan of ladder stands and ther’s zero chance you’ll ever find me in one of those climbers. I just don’t trust equipment that’s being left out all season and those climbers scare the crap out of me. Plus, I prefer the mobility of saddle hunting, especially somewhere that’s new to me.

Scouting is limited in this program, and without much time to scout before the season, it’s hard to know where I should set up. I want the flexibility to choose a suitable tree once I have a better idea of where I need to be. Every time I’ve gone into the woods, I’ve picked a different tree to climb because my awareness of what the deer are doing has improved. When I was scouting this season, I found what looked like a high traffic deer highway that followed an elevation contour and then crossed over a point. Literally the stuff you hear and read about. After two sits I realized the location was dead and moved on.

There’s also the cost. If you’re using stands, you need one stand for each location which adds up fast. It’s also hard and noisy to move them mid season if you realize a location isn’t producing like you originally thought it would.

With that in mind, here’s what I’m using, what I like about it, and what I’m still figuring out.

The saddle and storage pockets

My saddle is the Tethrd Phantom XP, and I love it. So far, I have zero complaints about the saddle itself. I also have Tethrd knee pads, which stay in my pack with the saddle until I get to my tree. The saddle is quite comfortable. I haven’t yet sat in it all day, but I have sat in it for four hours. To be clear, I prefer to stand or lean. I find sitting to be hard on my knees and legs and I think I move a lot more when I’m sitting. But, no matter which position I’m in, I’m quite comfortable.

This is a new setup for me. The saddle was just released this year. At this point, I think I have it mostly dialed in. I have a Tethrd SYS Hauler ES2.0 pocket on each side, one for my lineman’s rope and one for my tether.

On the left side I keep my lineman’s belt attached to the saddle and stowed in the SYS Hauler. Under the lineman’s belt is the cam buckle and strap for the platform. This works because my lineman’s belt is out of the pocket and wrapped around the tree whenever I need to retrieve or stow the platform strap.

On the right side I keep the tether. Under the tether is the HYS strap. Similar to the other pocket setup, any time the HYS strap needs to be retrieved or stowed the tether is wrapped around the tree.

I have two small complaints about the pockets. First, the drawstring closures make getting the ropes back in a little fiddly. Second, the smaller pockets on the outside aren’t deep enough to securely hold my HYS strap or the platform’s cam buckle and strap. This is why I put them under the ropes. It works, but I’d rather have dedicated pockets for these items.

Why I chose the Latitude ropes

I use the Latitude 8mm Vapor Line lineman’s belt and tree tether. The feature that sold me on them was the included prusik tender.

A prusik is a friction hitch used to adjust the rope system. The tender helps move that hitch along the rope with one hand. That one-handed adjustment is the convenience I wanted. Latitude supplies both the lineman’s belt and tree tether configured for single-handed operation.

A lot of folks use a mechanical ascender for this purpose, such as the Kong Duck, which replaces the prusik knot. For me, the appeal to the prusik tender is avoiding an additional mechanical device, along with the extra metal that could make noise or add weight.

The Predator V platform

The Tethrd Predator V is a little small for my liking once I’m up in the tree. I’d prefer more room underfoot. I wear a size 12.5, so I find the platform a little uncomfortable at times. I do a lot of rock climbing, so I’m used to having only a small part of my foot on a hold, but it can be clunky and difficult to turn around stealthily to get an angle on an unsuspecting deer.

On the walk in, though, that size is useful. It fits inside my First Lite Transfer pack, and I’m not sure how I’d fit a larger platform in there with everything else. The pack is designed so that you can remove the it from the frame and sandwich a platform between the frame and pack. However, it’s clearly meant for larger platforms, like the Timber Ninja Kunai. I don’t know if the extra-large Predator V will be big enough, but I’ll certainly provide updates when I get a chance to try it.

The Predator V is also a little loud and needs Stealth Strips to quiet it down. This is normal for metal platforms and sticks, but worth noting as the industry starts releasing things made of carbon.

One of the most important factors for a platform is stability. It’s all well and good to make a platform that sticks when you’re just standing on it. A platform for saddle hunting needs to be stable no matter what angle you’re kicking it from. If I lean out to my left, hanging from the tether and resting my feet against the left side of the platform, I need to know that it’s not going to kick out on me. Thus far, the Predator V has not dissapointed me. As I’ve gotten more familiar with deploying it, it’s gotten even more stable.

The Carbon Synergy climbing sticks

Safety update, November 3, 2026: Latitude issued a stop-use notice for all Carbon Synergy climbing sticks on November 2 after reports of steps slipping or detaching from the posts. The company instructs owners to stop using them immediately and offers a full refund regardless of where they were purchased. The observations below describe my experience before this notice.

My Latitude Carbon Synergy climbing sticks have been great to climb with. They’re extremely light, feel sturdy, and have plenty of grip.

One of my favorite things about them is the sound. If they knock into something, the carbon construction gives them more of a rattling-antler sound. They don’t have that metal twang.

Getting them attached to the tree is easy. Getting the attachment cord loose during teardown is harder. I haven’t quite figured out the trick to that part yet.

I either carry the sticks by hand or strap them underneath the Transfer pack. The pack has MOLLE-like webbing on the bottom, so I rigged up straps using one-inch webbing, tri-glides, and G-hooks. That gives me a way to carry the sticks on the pack when I want my hands free.

Carrying the sticks up the tree

For carrying the sticks while climbing, I borrowed an idea from Latitude. I tied a piece of 550 paracord to a loop of MOLLE webbing on my saddle using a double anchor knot. At the other end, I made a loop with a slip knot. That loop slips over the end of a step, letting the stack of sticks hang from the saddle.

This has worked well, but the sticks swing freely. I like that they generally hang clear of my other gear. The downside is that they can make noise if they swing into something. I’d like them to hang a little tighter to me so they don’t move around as much.

The next variation I want to try is tying a loop around one of the sticks and clipping it to an S-biner that lives on my saddle. I haven’t tested that arrangement yet, but I want to see whether it keeps the stack closer and cuts down on the swinging.

Hanging the bow

I use the Tethrd HYS strap with the Tethrd Fold N Go to hold my bow once I’m in the tree. It’s another part of the setup that packs away for the walk in, with the HYS strap currently living underneath a rope in one of the SYS Haulers.

Why I carry the Transfer pack

The First Lite Transfer is a little big and heavy. It’s probably more pack than many people would carry for this kind of hunt, but it works well for how I want to enter these parks.

I want to look as inconspicuous as possible. For me, that means everything goes in or on the pack. I strap my bow to it, keep my midlayers and insulation inside, and pack the saddle and knee pads instead of wearing them on the walk in. Those go on when I reach my tree.

Along with the hunting setup, I carry:

  • A tourniquet and small first aid kit
  • Extra 550 paracord and a carabiner
  • A kill kit
  • Snacks and a Nalgene for all-day sits

Between that gear, the platform, and the clothing, the space gets used. The pack’s size makes more sense when you consider that it’s carrying the saddle and layers as well as the things I need during the hunt.

What I am still adjusting

The saddle itself has been great. The things I’m still working on are mostly about packing, organization, and teardown: where the straps belong, how to make repacking the ropes less fiddly, and how to release the sticks’ attachment cords more easily. The platform needs quieting down, and I’d still like more room to stand.

For now, this setup gives me the mobility I wanted. I can carry everything to my tree, get set up there, and take the same equipment with me when I need to hunt somewhere else.

Sweet Soy Salmon

Sweet soy salmon grilled on a cedar plank.
By James Cacioppo | Tuesday, September 26, 2023

I decided to change my cedar plank salmon up

And I figured this was a great time to share. You know that salmon filet at the grocery store that comes on a cedar plank with a little tub of seasoning? You throw it on the grill, toss the seasoning on the salmon, and the brown sugar melts and caramelizes all over the salmon while the cedar plank imparts its own notes. It’s wonderful. Salmon in general is wonderful. You can brine and cold smoke it, brine and hot smoke it, skip right to smoking it, grill it, use lemon and pepper, garlic and butter… the possibilities are limitless.

The easy button in my house used to be the brown sugar option until my son decided that was too sweet. That means the maple syrup recipe I have will be too sweet as well. So, tonight I changed it up.

The ingredients (if you can call them that)

  • 1 1/2 tsp salt
  • fresh ground pepper
  • 1/2 cup prepared glaze (1/4 cup melted butter mixed with 1/4 cup Kikkoman Sweet Soy Glaze)
  • 2 lbs wild caught salmon
  • Nevermind those scrumptious little shrimps over there. These are not the shrimp you’re looking for.
Salt

Use whatever salt you want. This pink salt was on my counter top and I liked that it was a bit more coarse than Morton’s salt.

Warning from a marine biologist

Farm raised fish are pumped full of hormones. Genetically engineered farm raised fish take about half as long to reach maturity and full size compared to their ocean counterparts. I’m sure all the crap that’s pumped into them are within FDA regulated limits, but still.

Maybe stick with wild caught salmon.

Cook method

We’re grilling over an open flame. For this, I’m using the Yoder Smokers YS640S Pellet Grill because it has searing grates which can be placed directly over the open fire box turning this smoker into a wood fired grill.

I tossed some shrimp on for a snack while the rest of the food cooked. FYI, normally I’d aim for pulling the shrimp at 120 deg. Anyway…

Steps

  1. Soak the planks. You want to make sure the planks are good and soaked so they don’t catch fire. Start this first to make sure the wood planks have had adequate time to hydrate.
  2. Rinse and pat dry the salmon filet.
  3. Salt the filets. Hold the salt about a foot above the meat to get a nice and even sprinkle.
  4. Add pepper, ideally with a pepper grinder, to taste. Alternatively, you can toast whole peppercorns to make them aromatic and then crush them.
  5. Add the filets to the planks and toss on a grill pre-heated to between 400-600 deg
  6. Glaze the filets and cook, lid down, until the salmon reach 165 deg internal

Eat

Dish up some salmon with a vegetable and starch of your choice, and enjoy!

Coffee Woes

Why becoming a home barista can ruin coffee for you.
By James Cacioppo | Sunday, October 09, 2022

The BLUF

If you walk into a coffee shop and see these red flags, do yourself a favor and just get a drip coffee.

The long version

As the fall season kicks into gear I find myself stopping by coffee shops along the bike trail more often. That little mid-ride treat becomes almost essential around the 30 mile mark. Serious cyclists know the value of caffeine dosing. It’s a major reason for the caffeine content in Gu. Nothing replaces the body warming feeling of a hot cup of coffee on a cold day. Those 60+ mile rides can be as much of a mental challenge as they are physical so little pick-me-ups can make all of the difference.

Each time I’ve stopped in a coffee shop, however, I’ve been horribly disappointed and have learned to look out for a few key indicators before choosing between an espresso based drink and a drip coffee. Save yourself some money and heart ache by watching for these red flags!

Red flag 1: Portafilter mishandling

The portafilter is a chrome plated hunk of brass or bronze. The really high end ones are solid stainless steel. Why? Because that makes it a large thermal mass. Thermal mass, or thermal capacitance provides inertia against temperature fluctuations. You see, the name of the game in espresso is consistency (generally speaking, though there are some exceptions but that’s usually more to do with pressure and flow profiling and is way outside the scope of this post). The group head is tied to the boiler, either by a thermo-siphon, or by physical proximity. Either way, this is why espresso machines take a long time to warm up, because you have to let the group head match the temp of the boiler and the group head is also a massive chunck of metal. Then, that big hunk of metal called a portafilter sits in that group head and equalizes temperature. The idea here is that the water is always the right temp from the moment it leaves the boiler all the way into the cup. This is important for consistent extraction. Leaving the portafilter on the group head keeps the area where water and coffee mix the right temperature. All of that thermal capacity helps keep everything stable.

So how does one “mishandle” a portafilter? Leave it out of the group head or rinse it with tap water. Ideally, the barista will remove the portalfilter, quickly prep the puck, pull a shot, knock the puck out of the portafilter, wipe it down with a towel, and place it back in the group head. If a rinse is really needed, do it with brew water out of the brew head.

If you walk into a coffee shop and see the portalfilter just hanging around sitting on a counter or on the drip plate, do yourself a favor and just order a drip coffee. If you see the barista do something like rinse the portafilter in the sink, skip the espresso based beverage and grab a drip coffee.

Red flag 2: Dosing

1:2, 20 grams in and 40 grams out (18g in and 36g out for smaller portafilter baskets). That’s a great place to start for any roast and it’s where I point people when they’re starting out. What it means is you put 20 grams of ground coffee into the portafilter and you pull the shot until you have 40 grams of espresso in your cup. Consistency is key, remember? So if you stick to a 20g dose and adjust your grind so you get 40g out in 18-20s you should be in a great place.

In the old days they used to fill the portafilter until it was heaping over the top then level it by knocking the excess off with a swipe of their finger. Super precise. They would also base their shot purely on time. In fact, many commercial machines still have programable single and double shots based on time. Really good baristas can still make this work but the chances of getting a bad shot are much higher. The time based shot should still get it in the ballpark provided everything else is consistent, but that dosing method of old has to be let out to pasture.

These days, commercial grinders come with built in scales. You pop the portafilter into the holder, press it to activate the grinder, and the grinder just does its thing and shuts off at the preset weight. Even if a shop has older grinders that don’t operate by weight, external scales are cheap and plentiful these days. There’s simply no excuse for playing the old guessing game anymore.

So watch the barista when you walk into the next shop. If you see a doser and no scale, just ask for a drip coffee.

Red flag 3: Puck prep

When you pull that lever (or push the button) water hits that puck at a pressure of 9 bar. That’s about 130psi. There needs to be enough resistance such that it takes 20-ish seconds for 40 grams of water to pass. Moreover, you want that water to evenly saturate the whole puck and pass over all of the grinds rather than channel through it in a few specific spots. If channeling happens, the grinds in the channel will be over extracted while the rest will be under extracted.

Again, we find ourselves discussing consistency. The grind needs to be consistent, the distribution of grinds must be consistent, and the compactness across the puck has to be consistent. So when you walk in you should see at least two things: a burr grinder and a distribution tool. In the best case you’ll also see a nice tamper but there’s a lot of debate amongst the best baristas as to whether or not the tamper still has a place.

What you don’t want to see is clumpy mounds of coffee grinds piling into a portafilter and then a barista just throwing a tamper on top and pressing away. That’s just a recipe for uneven distribution and channeling galore.

Red flag 4: Milk steaming

Milk steaming is a whole art form of itself. It’s almost as if the first art one must master is espresso and the second art is steaming milk.

Milk is fickle. There’s a narrow, about 10 degree Fahrenheit, window where the milk takes on a natural sweetness. Any lower or higher and that sweetness is lost. It doesn’t take long to get there either. My Rocket Espresso Mozzafiato will steam a six ounce pitcher in about 18 seconds. Frothing, or texturing, should be done at the very beginning and will only take about three seconds on any machine with a dedicated steam boiler. The whole thing is a very quick process and most baristas will feel the temperature with one hand on the bottom and side of the pitcher.

What I see all too often is a barista over-aerate the milk for a few seconds, then set the pitcher down and let it continue to steam while they do something else. If you see this, walk away.

Pushing Documentation to the Left

Why you should push documentation to the left on your projects
By James Cacioppo | Saturday, October 08, 2022

I spent Friday night and Saturday morning falling down the rabbit hole of broken docs. Every time I update one code snippet I stumble on another that’s broken. I’m not mad. I enjoy documentation. I’m broken, I know, but I really do enjoy it. There’s no better way to solidify your knowledge on a subject than to teach others. The medical field used to have a saying: “See one, do one, teach one.”

Whether or not I’m enjoying wasting my Saturday morning fixing broken docs is irrelevant. I’m here because I got an email from a data service provider my customer has told to integrate with my application. This individual innocently and politely asked for help as they were attempting to use our example in our docs and it wasn’t working. After reviewing, I had to inform them in front of my customer that our docs are out of date and that he needed to change his commands.

It was a bad look and it can be embarrassing. When a dev team leaves the task of documentation for after the feature development, documentation often gets forgotten or pushed to the right as more pressing matters come up. This trend continues until one day you find that more than half of your docs are out of date.

The solution is to push documentation to the left! When are updated docs on a feature needed? When that feature code goes live. However, if you wait until the code goes live to publish the updated docs then you’re already behind. For this reason, we’ve implemented a rule that documentation must be completed before the story can be moved to done, the bug ticket can be resolved, and the PR can be merged. In other words, documentation is always part of the acceptance criteria. This adds extra layers of accountability as now every reviewer is also responsible for making sure that documentation is complete before approving.

Pushing documentation to the left is, like all other things, a practice of taking a little more pain up front to avoid a lot later. Give it a try!
